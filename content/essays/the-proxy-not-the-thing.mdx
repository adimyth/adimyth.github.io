[PitchWiz](https://pitchwiz.ai/) is a real-time conversational AI platform for sales training. A rep joins a video call with an agent playing a customer, works through a pitch or a round of objections, and at the end the system scores them. Did they cover the points they were supposed to cover. How did they handle the pushback. How clearly did they communicate.

That score goes to the rep. It also goes to their manager.

We run it in twelve languages. When I pulled the average score by language, it was not flat. Malayalam sessions averaged 70.3. Hindi and English sat around 67. Kannada was 59.2. Assamese, on the smallest sample we had, was 52.7.

An eighteen point spread by language is the kind of thing you have to explain. And there is an explanation available immediately, which is the problem.

## The hypothesis that should worry you

Our speech recognition is not equally good in every language. We measure that, carefully, with a benchmark built for the purpose. So the question writes itself: **are users of the languages we transcribe worst also the users who score worst?**

If yes, that is not a metric curiosity. It means the pipeline mishears somebody, the model marks them down for something they actually said, and a number lands in their performance review. Nobody in the loop can see it happen. The rep sees a low score. The manager sees a low score. The transcript looks fine, because the transcript is the thing that is wrong.

So I ran it. Per-language word error rate against per-language average score, ten languages present in both datasets.

**Pearson r of -0.70. p = 0.024.**

I want to be honest about what that felt like, because it is the whole point of this essay. It felt like a finding. Negative, sizeable, significant at the conventional threshold, and pointed at exactly the uncomfortable conclusion you would expect a careful team to reach. It is the sort of number that goes in a slide with the word *fairness* above it.

## It does not survive

Ten data points is not many. When you have ten of something, the cheapest check available is to remove one and see what happens. So: drop each language in turn, recompute, and look at the column.

I ran it against all four of the speech recognition models we had benchmarked, because I could not establish from the data which one had actually served those sessions.

**Remove one language and the correlation goes from -0.70 to zero.**

That language is Assamese, which is simultaneously the worst transcribed and the lowest scoring, on 471 sessions. It is one point sitting in the corner of the scatter plot, and it was holding up the entire result by itself.

Three other checks say the same thing in different accents. Spearman's rank correlation is **-0.24**, where Pearson was -0.70. When those two diverge that hard, Pearson is being dragged by leverage from an extreme point rather than describing any consistent relationship, and the ranks simply do not line up. Weighting by session count roughly halves the coefficient, which means whatever relationship exists lives in the languages with the least data. And the coefficient ranges from -0.70 to -0.22 depending only on *which model's* error rate you use, so the independent variable was never really pinned down at all.

## You did not need any of that

Here is the part that stings. Once you stop looking at the correlation and look at the rows, the story falls apart without a single statistic.

**Kannada has the best transcription accuracy of any language in our fleet, at 3.91% word error rate, and the second worst average score.** Tamil has close to the worst transcription and a perfectly ordinary score. Malayalam has near-best transcription and the best score in the set.

Three rows. No p-value. If somebody had read the table before running the correlation, there would have been nothing to run.

## It was doomed before the arithmetic

Even if the coefficient had held, the measurement was never going to support the claim, for reasons that have nothing to do with statistics.

**The error rate was not measured on these sessions.** It comes from a golden set of twenty sentences per language, read aloud by two speakers into a clean microphone. Read speech is a different problem from spontaneous conversation over a video call with a nervous salesperson talking fast. The benchmark is a good instrument for comparing vendors. It is not a measurement of how well we heard anybody in production.

**Language is confounded with everything else.** A different language means a different client, a different product, a different set of points to be covered, and a different population of salespeople with different training. Assamese at 471 sessions is almost certainly one customer. I was not comparing languages. I was comparing customers, and calling it language.

**And it is a claim about individuals built from group averages.** Even a real relationship between a language's average error rate and its average score would say nothing about whether any particular rep was marked down for being misheard.

So the chain ran: *how well did we hear this user* to *how well do we hear this language* to *how well did a benchmark hear two volunteers reading sentences*. Three steps away from the thing, and each step looked reasonable on its own.

## Once you know the shape, you find it everywhere

This is not a story about one bad correlation. It is a story about a category of error I then started seeing across the whole evaluation stack. **The metric that hurts you is almost never the wrong one. It is the stable, reproducible, respectable one, pointed slightly to the left of the thing you care about.** Wrong metrics get caught. Adjacent ones survive review for years.

**Word error rate itself is adjacent.** It counts every word equally. A dropped postposition costs exactly as much as a wrong premium amount. In an insurance pitch the premium amount *is* the content and the postposition is noise, so the rate answers a question nobody asked. This is why the most useful column in our transcription reports is not the rate at all. It is the diff: a per-row list of what changed, rendered as `reference phrase -> transcript phrase`. A word error rate of 12% is not actionable. A column showing you that the product name got mangled is.

**And it can break outright.** One vendor we benchmarked returns transcripts that mix scripts, writing English loanwords in Latin characters inside an otherwise Devanagari sentence. That is arguably *more* faithful to how the sentence was spoken, since that is exactly how people talk here. Measured against a fully Devanagari reference, it scores terribly. The metric was built for languages where a sentence is written in one script, and real Indian speech is not that. We could not report a number for that vendor, so we did not report one. The norm is to publish the bad number with a footnote and let it quietly define the vendor's reputation.

**And a metric can be valid at one altitude and invalid at another.** When we evaluated swapping the model behind our scoring, the two models ranked sessions almost identically and scored them about ten points apart. The obvious fix is to add ten. That is genuinely correct if the number's job is to feed a dashboard, and after calibration the aggregates line up. **But individual sessions still differed by ten to fifteen points.** Aligned on average, wrong per person. Which is fine for a report and disqualifying for a number attached to somebody's name.

## One I have not resolved

Sessions where the agent is configured with a neutral persona average 69.4. Sessions with a friendly persona average 59.9.

**A 9.5 point gap, produced by a configuration setting.**

There are two explanations and they demand opposite responses. Either the friendly persona is genuinely a harder customer to sell to, in which case that is a product insight and the scores are working. Or the same rubric behaves differently when the conversational partner behaves differently, in which case a configuration flag is silently moving people's assessments and has been for a while.

Nobody has checked which. I am including it because it is the same shape as everything above and because I do not get to only publish the ones I resolved.

## Where the instinct was right

The counterweight, so this does not read as though everything was broken.

We have no ground truth for text to speech. There is no correct waveform, so the only instrument is human listening, and the rubric we built has nine dimensions in three tiers. The third tier is the interesting one: **numbers and dates, foreign word handling, technical term accuracy.**

Nobody derives those three from a paper. They are there because somebody sat and listened to a lot of synthesised Indian-language audio and noticed that the sentence holds together beautifully right up until it hits a rupee figure or an English product name embedded in a Telugu clause, and then falls over. Real speech here is code-mixed, and code-mixing is precisely where these models break.

**The dimensions are the finding. The one-to-five is just how you write it down.** The same instinct shows up in our retrieval benchmark, where the question categories include "overlapping queries" and "queries spanning multiple documents". Those are not topics. They are failure modes wearing a topic's clothes, and somebody put them there after watching retrieval fail in exactly those two ways.

That is what a metric looks like when it is pointed at the thing instead of next to it. You get there by looking at outputs, not by picking a scale.

## What would actually answer the original question

I still do not know whether we mishear some users into lower scores. The correlation did not answer it and no amount of repair to that correlation will, because the unit is wrong. Language-level averages cannot answer a question about people.

The fix is to change the unit rather than the statistic. Join at the session level, inside a single client and a single roleplay, so the content, the product and the sales population are held fixed and the only thing varying is how well we heard that particular person on that particular call.

Two per-session signals already exist and neither needs new instrumentation.

- **Sentence-level transcription confidence.** We already compute it, because it drives an automatic fallback: when confidence drops below that language's threshold, the audio is reprocessed through a different vendor. It is a real quality measurement, taken on the actual audio, of the actual session.
- **Clarification request count.** How often the agent asked the user to repeat themselves, detected by a plain regex taxonomy over the transcript. No model call, no reference needed, computable on every session we have ever run. It is a behavioural proxy for the pipeline failing to understand somebody, which is much closer to the thing than a benchmark is.

Regress session score on those two, with client and roleplay as fixed effects. If the effect is real it will show up there, and it will mean something.

And one cheap addition that has been sitting available the whole time: compute an **entity error rate** from the diff column we already generate, counting only numbers, dates, currency and product names. Those are the tokens the scoring rubric actually rewards. Word error rate weights them the same as everything else, which is the original sin restated.

## The check took four lines

I would have published the -0.70. It was significant, it fit a hypothesis worth taking seriously, and it flattered us: it made the team look like it was the sort of team that goes looking for fairness problems in its own product.

The thing that killed it was four lines of code that drop one row and recompute. Not sophistication. Not a review process. A habit.

**The number was not the finding. The willingness to try to break it was.**
